Tempo is the speed of the music, measured in **beats per minute (BPM)**. Before the metronome, composers indicated speed with Italian words: *Largo*, *Andante*, *Allegro*. Those words describe a character more than an exact number, which is why every source gives slightly different ranges. From 1815, with the mechanical metronome patented by Johann Maelzel, scores also started to carry a numeric marking such as "♩ = 120", meaning one hundred and twenty quarter notes per minute.

This page gathers the traditional tempo chart and a practical guide, speed by speed, from 40 to 200 BPM. Each section has a link that opens the [online metronome](/en/) already set to that BPM.

## Tempo markings chart

| Marking | BPM | Character and examples |
|---|---|---|
| Larghissimo | up to 24 | Extremely slow. Rare; used for effects and in some contemporary pieces. |
| Grave | 25 – 45 | Heavy, solemn. Slow introductions of symphonies and funeral marches. |
| Lento | 45 – 50 | Slow. Contemplative pieces. |
| Largo | 50 – 60 | Broad, spacious. Handel's "Largo", very slow ballads. |
| Adagio | 60 – 70 | Calm, at ease. Slow movements of sonatas and concertos. |
| Adagietto | 70 – 85 | A little more moving than Adagio. Pop and rock ballads, reggae. |
| Andante | 85 – 97 | "Walking", walking pace. Samba, soul, classic hip-hop. |
| Moderato | 97 – 109 | Moderate. Pop, classic rock, Brazilian sertanejo. |
| Allegretto | 109 – 132 | Lively, unhurried. House, dance pop, forró, Brazilian funk. |
| Allegro | 132 – 140 | Fast and cheerful. Axé, trance, fast rock. |
| Vivace | 140 – 177 | Lively, brilliant. Punk, ska, bluegrass, drum and bass. |
| Presto | 177 – 240 | Very fast. Viennese waltz in quarter notes, bebop, extreme metal. |

Besides these, tempo-change expressions appear often: *ritardando* (getting slower), *accelerando* (getting faster), *a tempo* (back to the original speed) and *rubato* (with freedom of tempo, "stealing" time from one note to give to another).

## How to choose the BPM for practice

The song's BPM is the destination, not the starting point. To practice a new passage, the safest rule is to start between 50% and 70% of the final tempo, at a speed where you can play with no mistakes, and go up 4 or 5 BPM after each clean repetition. The full method is in the article [How to practice with a metronome](/en/how-to-practice-with-a-metronome/).

It is also worth remembering that the same BPM can be felt in different ways. A song at 160 BPM counted in quarter notes is the same song at 80 BPM counted in half notes. That is why many musicians prefer to set the metronome at half the tempo and feel each click as two beats. The sections below have several suggestions of that kind.

## Guide by speed

### 40 BPM: the tempo of control {#40-bpm}

40 BPM is the start of the **Grave** range, one of the slowest tempos used in musical practice. Each beat lasts 1.5 seconds. It is the tempo at which violinists practice long bows and pianists work on arm weight in every note.

For most musicians, 40 BPM is an **exercise** tempo, not a repertoire tempo. Practicing a difficult passage here exposes every detail: the note that comes out weaker, the finger that arrives late, the breath that does not fit.

**Tip:** with the pulse so spread out it is easy to lose the reference. Turn on eighth-note or sixteenth-note subdivisions, so the metronome fills the time and you can put each note in its place. When you feel secure, turn the subdivision off and try to hold the time by yourself between clicks.

[Open the metronome at 40 BPM](/en/?bpm=40)

### 50 BPM: Largo {#50-bpm}

50 BPM sits on the border between **Lento** and **Largo**. It is the tempo of solemn openings, hymns sung calmly and many ballads played feeling the time in long quarter notes.

In the practice room, 50 BPM is the classic starting point for those who follow the rule of starting between 50% and 70% of the final tempo. A passage that needs to reach 100 BPM starts here, with every note heard and corrected.

For fingerpicking on guitar and arpeggios on piano, use 50 BPM with one note per click on the first day, two notes per click (eighth notes) on the second and four (sixteenths) on the third. The BPM does not change, but the density doubles at each step.

[Open the metronome at 50 BPM](/en/?bpm=50)

### 60 BPM: one click per second {#60-bpm}

60 BPM is the easiest tempo to feel: **one beat per second**, the same rhythm as a clock. That is why it is the first BPM teachers recommend to anyone who has never practiced with a metronome. It sits at the start of the **Adagio** range.

It is also the tempo of many ballads and slow songs, of sacred repertoire and of a large part of the slow pieces of the Baroque and Classical periods. A resting heart beats close to this, between 60 and 100 times per minute.

In practice, 60 BPM is the base for chord changes on guitar, rudiments on drums and sight-reading. In 4/4, one bar lasts four seconds, enough time to think about the next note. When the passage is clean, go up to [70](#70-bpm) and [80 BPM](#80-bpm).

[Open the metronome at 60 BPM](/en/?bpm=60)

### 70 BPM: Adagietto {#70-bpm}

70 BPM is a calm tempo, at the start of the **Adagietto** range. It is at the heart of rock and pop ballads, traditional reggae and much slow country and sertanejo. In many recordings the 70 BPM pulse is felt with the snare on beats 2 and 4, which gives that feeling of music that "breathes".

For practice, 70 BPM is a good second step after [60 BPM](#60-bpm). There is still time to correct every note, but the passage already starts to flow. Wind players use this tempo to work on long phrases and breath control.

**Tip:** at tempos like this, try feeling the click as an eighth note, that is, consider that the music is at 140 BPM with the metronome marking only half. This change of reference helps to understand the difference between "half-time" and "double-time".

[Open the metronome at 70 BPM](/en/?bpm=70)

### 80 BPM: the comfort zone {#80-bpm}

80 BPM closes the **Adagietto** range and is one of the most frequent tempos in popular music. Many pop and rock ballads, bossa nova counted in 2/4, R&B and classic hip-hop (usually between 80 and 95 BPM) live in this region.

It is a comfortable tempo for reading and for most technical exercises. Fast enough for the passage to flow, slow enough to keep control. Many musicians use 80 BPM as a "warm-up tempo" at the start of practice.

On drums and guitar, 80 BPM is great for practicing grooves with sixteenth-note subdivision: four notes per click still fit comfortably and syncopations stay clear. To move on, go to [90](#90-bpm) and then [100 BPM](#100-bpm).

[Open the metronome at 80 BPM](/en/?bpm=80)

### 90 BPM: Andante, walking tempo {#90-bpm}

**Andante** comes from the Italian "walking", and 90 BPM is literally a relaxed walking pace. It is a very present tempo in Brazilian music: samba and pagode usually sit between 90 and 110 BPM counted in 2/4, with the syncopations happening inside each beat.

In American funk, soul and hip-hop, 90 BPM gives the "heavy groove": the snare on beats 2 and 4 lands with weight and the kick has room for variations.

For practice, 90 BPM is a good transition tempo between the full control of slow tempos and the flow of fast ones. Use 2 beats per bar for rhythms in 2/4 and turn on sixteenth notes to see the syncopations of samba.

[Open the metronome at 90 BPM](/en/?bpm=90)

### 100 BPM: Moderato {#100-bpm}

100 BPM is a round number and a reference tempo: the start of the **Moderato** range, neither slow nor fast. It is the speed of many pop songs, of livelier samba, of part of Brazilian sertanejo and of much of the calmer classic rock. It is also the recommended rate for CPR (between 100 and 120 compressions per minute).

In technique practice, 100 BPM is a milestone: scales in sixteenth notes at 100 BPM mean almost seven notes per second. Anyone who gets here cleanly already has the base for most of the repertoire.

**Tip:** compare the same passage at [80](#80-bpm), 100 and [120 BPM](#120-bpm). If quality drops a lot from one to the next, the previous tempo still needs consolidating.

[Open the metronome at 100 BPM](/en/?bpm=100)

### 110 BPM: Allegretto {#110-bpm}

110 BPM opens the **Allegretto** range: it already has energy, but without the rush of fast tempos. It is the territory of the hotter samba de roda, of baião and forró pé de serra, which usually sit between 110 and 130 BPM with the accent on the offbeat. In pop and rock, 110 BPM is a very common tempo for songs with a strong chorus.

For practice, 110 BPM is usually the point where sixteenth-note subdivisions start to demand real technique. If a scale in sixteenths gets tangled here, go back to [100 BPM](#100-bpm) and go up 4 at a time.

On guitar, 110 BPM is a good tempo for consolidating strumming patterns with down and up strokes on every eighth note.

[Open the metronome at 110 BPM](/en/?bpm=110)

### 120 BPM: the default tempo {#120-bpm}

120 BPM is probably the most used tempo in the world. It is the factory default of almost every music production software, the reference speed of military marches (120 steps per minute) and the base of dance floor electronic music: house and dance pop live between 120 and 128 BPM. In the traditional classification, it sits in the middle of the **Allegretto** range.

Exactly two clicks per second: easy to feel, easy to double or halve. That is why it is also a good tempo for switching between the feeling of "half-time" (feeling the click at 60) and "double-time" (at 240).

In practice, 120 BPM is the goal of many technical exercises: scales in sixteenth notes at 120 BPM are already eight notes per second. Those chasing speed go on to [130](#130-bpm), [140](#140-bpm) and beyond.

[Open the metronome at 120 BPM](/en/?bpm=120)

### 130 BPM: energy without hurry {#130-bpm}

130 BPM closes the **Allegretto** range and is a high-energy tempo in dance music. Brazilian funk revolves around 130 BPM, an inheritance from Miami bass; techno and much of the more intense electronic music sit between 125 and 135; a lot of upbeat rock and pop punk also live here.

For instrumentalists, 130 BPM is where sixteenth notes start to demand economy of motion. You can no longer "force": the hand needs to relax and the gesture gets smaller. If the passage locks up, do not insist here. Go back to [110](#110-bpm) or [120 BPM](#120-bpm) and climb again in small steps.

**Tip:** try 130 BPM with only 2 beats per bar to feel the rhythm in 2/4, as in Brazilian marchinha and frevo.

[Open the metronome at 130 BPM](/en/?bpm=130)

### 140 BPM: Allegro {#140-bpm}

140 BPM is **Allegro** par excellence: fast, cheerful, with drive. It is the typical tempo of axé and many electronic genres, such as trance (between 135 and 140) and dubstep, which is counted at 140 BPM but felt at half. In rock, 140 BPM is where the really fast songs begin.

In technique practice, 140 BPM is an important milestone. Sixteenth notes at 140 BPM exceed nine notes per second. Anyone who plays a clean scale here already has the speed needed for most rock, pop and Brazilian repertoire.

**Tip:** at fast tempos, sixteenth-note subdivision gets confusing to hear. Use eighth notes or no subdivision and let the feeling of speed come from the music, not from the click.

[Open the metronome at 140 BPM](/en/?bpm=140)

### 150 BPM: Vivace {#150-bpm}

150 BPM enters the **Vivace** range, lively and brilliant. It is the tempo of much 70s and 80s rock, of ska, pop punk and various marching band pieces. It also appears in fast ballroom dances when counted in quarter notes.

For practice, 150 BPM is a tempo at which time control is as much physical as mental. Small tensions in the hand or arm turn into delay. The recommendation is the same as for the previous tempos, only stricter: the passage must come out clean at [140 BPM](#140-bpm) several times in a row before arriving here.

**Tip:** at Vivace tempos, many musicians prefer to feel the click in 2 beats per bar (half the tempo), which keeps internal time at a more comfortable pulse of 75 BPM.

[Open the metronome at 150 BPM](/en/?bpm=150)

### 160 BPM: real speed {#160-bpm}

At 160 BPM the music is already running. It is the tempo of punk rock, much hardcore and metal, bluegrass and part of traditional fast dance music. In electronic music it approaches drum and bass, which lives between 165 and 175. Still inside the **Vivace** range, but close to the border with Presto.

For instrumentalists, 160 BPM is the tempo where eighth notes already demand attention and sixteenth notes exceed ten notes per second. Drummers use 160 BPM to practice double bass and alternate strokes; guitarists, for gallops and tremolo.

**Tip:** from here on, practicing with the metronome marking only beats 1 and 3 (setting 80 BPM and feeling each click as two beats) is more useful than hearing clicks on every beat.

[Open the metronome at 160 BPM](/en/?bpm=160)

### 170 BPM: the limit of Vivace {#170-bpm}

170 BPM is at the top of the **Vivace** range, one step from Presto. It is the central tempo of drum and bass and jungle (between 165 and 175 BPM), of much thrash metal and of virtuoso pieces of the classical repertoire counted in quarter notes.

Curiously, 170 BPM is also a reference outside music: runners use a cadence of 170 to 180 steps per minute as an efficiency standard. Many running playlists are built exactly in that range.

In practice, few passages need to be played at 170 BPM in sixteenth notes. More commonly this tempo is used for fast eighth notes or to feel the half, 85 BPM, with subdivision. If you got here cleanly, congratulations: your technique is at an advanced level.

[Open the metronome at 170 BPM](/en/?bpm=170)

### 180 BPM: Presto {#180-bpm}

**Presto** starts at 177 BPM, and 180 BPM is its most used reference. It is the tempo of the Viennese waltz counted in quarter notes (three per bar, with the whole bar at 60), of faster drum and bass, of hardcore punk and of many bravura pieces in the piano and violin repertoire.

At 180 BPM there are three clicks per second. Hearing and following each click individually gets hard. It is the point where musicians start to feel time in groups: by bar, or two beats at a time. On the metronome, a good strategy is to set 3 beats per bar for a waltz or to use the accent on the first beat as the only strong reference.

For runners, 180 BPM is the reference cadence of many coaches, and a song at 180 BPM helps keep the pace.

[Open the metronome at 180 BPM](/en/?bpm=180)

### 200 BPM: Presto at the limit {#200-bpm}

200 BPM is an extreme tempo: more than three clicks per second, inside the **Presto** range and close to Prestissimo. It is the speed of the fastest bebop, with musicians like Charlie Parker playing heads at tempos close to 300; of extreme metal, of hardcore and of some classical pieces played in very fast quarter notes.

Practically nobody practices sixteenth notes at 200 BPM. The real use of this tempo is for eighth notes (walking bass in fast jazz, tremolo picking) or to feel the pulse in 2, that is, 100 BPM with the click in eighth notes.

**Tip:** if you need to practice something at this speed, set the metronome at [100 BPM](#100-bpm) with eighth-note subdivision or with 2 beats per bar. The sound result is the same and the reference is much easier to follow.

[Open the metronome at 200 BPM](/en/?bpm=200)
