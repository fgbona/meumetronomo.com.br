**BPM** stands for *beats per minute*. It is the unit that measures tempo, that is, the speed of a piece of music. A song at 60 BPM has one beat per second; at 120 BPM, two per second. The higher the number, the faster the music.

The "beat" in question is the pulse: the regular time that makes you tap your foot or nod your head. It is not every note, nor every stroke of the drummer. It is the basic unit that other durations divide or multiply.

## Where BPM appears

- **On sheet music**, as a metronome marking: "♩ = 100" means one hundred quarter notes per minute. When the note value is different (a half note, a dotted quarter), the math changes, so it matters which figure sits next to the number.
- **In recording and production software**, where the project BPM defines the bar grid, loops and synced effects. The factory default of most of them is 120 BPM.
- **In running and gym playlists**, built by BPM range to match the cadence of your steps.
- **On the metronome**, which plays exactly the number of beats per minute you choose. The [online metronome](/en/) goes from 1 to 240 BPM.

## How to count the BPM of a song

### With a stopwatch

Count the beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. For more precision, count for 30 seconds and multiply by 2, or count a full minute. Start counting from zero at the instant you start the stopwatch, not from one, or the result will be one beat too high.

Example: 23 beats in 15 seconds give 92 BPM. Since a 15-second count has a margin of error of about 4 BPM, repeat it once or twice and average.

### With tap tempo

The most practical way is the **Tap tempo** button on the metronome. Tap it on the pulse of the song, at least four times, and the metronome averages the intervals between taps. The more taps, the more precise the average. If you lose the pulse, stop for two seconds and start again: the count resets.

This method also works to find the tempo of a song that is only in your head, with no recording: sing it mentally and tap the button.

### Counting bars

If you know the song is in 4/4, you can count bars instead of beats: count how many bars fit in 30 seconds and multiply by 8. It is useful in fast songs, where counting every beat gets hard.

## Why the same song can have two BPMs

A common doubt: one site says the song is at 90 BPM and another says 180. Both may be right. What changes is which note value each one treated as the pulse.

This happens mainly in two cases:

- **Half-time and double-time.** In a lot of hip-hop, trap and dubstep, the hi-hat runs in sixteenth notes while the snare lands only once per bar. You can feel the fast pulse (the hi-hat) or the slow one (the snare). Trap, for example, is produced between 130 and 150 BPM but is almost always felt at half that, between 65 and 75.
- **Compound meters.** In 6/8, the natural pulse is the dotted quarter note (three eighth notes). Counting the eighth notes gives a number three times larger than counting the dotted quarters. A ballad in 6/8 can be "50 BPM" or "150 BPM" depending on what you count.

When in doubt, prefer the BPM at which the song "swings" most naturally, usually between 70 and 140. That is where most people feel the pulse.

## BPM and traditional tempo markings

Before the metronome existed, speed was indicated with Italian words: Largo, Andante, Allegro, Presto. They are still in use, and each corresponds to an approximate BPM range. The full table, with style examples for each range, is in the [tempo markings guide](/en/tempo-markings/).

## BPM ranges of common styles

| Style | Approximate BPM |
|---|---|
| Ballad, bossa nova | 60 – 80 |
| Hip-hop, R&B | 80 – 100 |
| Samba, pagode (in 2/4) | 90 – 110 |
| Reggae | 60 – 90 |
| Pop, rock | 100 – 140 |
| Country, sertanejo | 100 – 130 |
| Forró, baião | 110 – 130 |
| Brazilian funk | 125 – 135 |
| House, techno | 120 – 135 |
| Axé | 130 – 150 |
| Drum and bass | 165 – 175 |
| Punk, metal | 160 – 220 |

These numbers are averages. Within each style there are much slower and much faster songs, and nothing replaces counting the BPM of the specific song you want to play.

## BPM and heart rate

It is no coincidence that most songs sit between 60 and 140 BPM: that is the range of the human heart rate, from rest to moderate exercise. Songs around 60 to 70 BPM tend to calm; above 120, to energize. Running playlists usually stay between 160 and 180 BPM, the step cadence recommended by many coaches.

## Using BPM in practice

Knowing the BPM of a song is the first step to practicing it. The second is not to start there. The rule is to begin between 50% and 70% of the final tempo and go up little by little, 4 or 5 BPM at a time. The article [How to practice with a metronome](/en/how-to-practice-with-a-metronome/) describes the full method.
