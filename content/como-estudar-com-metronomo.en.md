The metronome is the cheapest and most effective tool for improving rhythmic precision. But it only works when used with a method. Practicing by "turning on the click and playing" helps little. What makes the difference is how you choose the tempo, split the passage and raise the speed.

This article describes the method instrument teachers have used for decades, adapted to the [online metronome](/en/). It works for any instrument and any level, from the beginner who still struggles to change chords to the musician preparing a virtuoso piece.

## 1. Start slowly, really slowly

Choose a BPM at which you can play the passage **with no mistakes at all**. For most difficult passages that is between 50% and 70% of the final tempo. If the song is at 120 BPM, starting at [60](/en/tempo-markings/#60-bpm) or [80 BPM](/en/tempo-markings/#80-bpm) is normal. Playing slowly and correctly teaches the body the right movement. Playing fast and wrong teaches the mistake.

"Slow" is usually slower than it seems. A simple test: if you need to think about the next note while playing the current one, the tempo is still too high. At the right BPM you have attention to spare for listening to the sound, watching your posture and relaxing your hand.

## 2. Short passages

Do not practice the whole song with the metronome. Pick two to eight bars, preferably the part that trips you up the most. Repeat only that passage until it comes out clean three times in a row. Then join it with the previous passage.

An efficient way to pick the passage is to play the whole song once without the metronome and note the bars where you hesitated. Those bars, and only those, are the day's practice material. The rest of the song is already good enough and does not need repetition.

## 3. Raise the tempo in small steps

Increase by 4 or 5 BPM at a time. If you make a mistake twice at the same tempo, go back 10 BPM and climb again. A typical session progression looks like 60, 64, 68, 72, 76, 80. In a week the difference is large, and you reach the final tempo without having "skipped" a step.

Write down the maximum clean BPM for each passage at the end of the session. The next day, start about 10 BPM below that value, not at it. The body forgets a little from one day to the next, and starting below makes sure the first repetition of the day already comes out right.

## 4. Use subdivisions at slow tempos

Below 60 BPM the gap between clicks gets long and it is easy to lose the reference. Turn on eighth-note or sixteenth-note subdivisions. They fill the time and make it clear where each note goes. When the passage is comfortable, turn the subdivisions off and try to keep the same precision with the pulse alone.

Subdivisions also help diagnose problems. If a sixteenth-note passage comes out uneven, turn on sixteenths in the metronome and play along: it becomes obvious which of the four notes in each beat is out of place. Usually it is the third or the fourth, the ones that fall "between" the main clicks.

## 5. Change the click reference

A technique used by professional musicians: set the metronome at **half** the tempo and feel the click only on beats 2 and 4, like a snare drum. Or reduce it to one click per bar. The less reference the metronome gives, the more your internal time has to work. If the click "disappears" and comes back in the right place, you are in time.

A possible progression for the same passage:

1. Click on every beat, with subdivision.
2. Click on every beat, without subdivision.
3. Click only on beats 2 and 4 (metronome at half the BPM).
4. One click per bar (metronome at a quarter of the BPM, 1 beat per bar).
5. No metronome, recording yourself to check afterwards.

## 6. Record yourself

Play with the metronome and record with your phone. Listen afterwards. Small delays and rushes that go unnoticed while we play become obvious in the recording. It is the most honest feedback there is.

When listening, pay attention to three moments where time tends to slip: the first note after a rest, the return after a difficult passage, and long notes, which tend to be cut short.

## A 15-minute routine

For those with little time, a short, organized session yields more than an hour without a plan:

| Minutes | What to do |
|---|---|
| 0 – 3 | Warm-up at a comfortable tempo: a scale or a familiar exercise, just to wake up the hands. |
| 3 – 10 | The day's difficult passage, starting 10 BPM below yesterday's maximum and going up 4 at a time. |
| 10 – 13 | The same passage with the click on beats 2 and 4, or one click per bar. |
| 13 – 15 | Play the passage in the context of the song, from the start of the previous phrase to the end of the next. |

The metronome's [timer](/en/) helps you keep to these blocks without watching the clock.

## Common mistakes

- **Raising the BPM too early.** If the passage only comes out "more or less", it is not time to speed up.
- **Ignoring the click.** Turning the metronome on and playing over it without listening. If that happens, lower the instrument's volume or raise the metronome's.
- **Practicing only fast.** Even those who already master the passage should return to the slow tempo now and then to clean up details.
- **Long unfocused sessions.** Fifteen minutes concentrated on one passage are worth more than an hour playing the song from start to finish.
- **Always playing together with the click.** When the metronome becomes a crutch, internal time does not develop. Alternate with the reduced references from section 5.
- **Changing the tempo mid-passage.** Choose the BPM, play the whole passage, only then adjust. Adjusting every bar removes the reference.
